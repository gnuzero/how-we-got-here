    Who Decides the Wire

    September 6, 2026

    ARPANET (Post 39) and TCP/IP (Post 41) solved how packets move. They did not, on their own, answer a quieter question: who gets to say what the bits mean? Port 80, an IPv4 prefix, .com, the code point for ก — none of those work unless distant operators treat the same identifiers the same way. The internet’s governance story is the story of that agreement: not a parliament over the network, but a set of shared ledgers for numbers, names, and encodings.

    Documents Before Institutions#

    The first coordination tool was a document series. In April 1969, Steve Crocker published RFC 1 — Host Software — as working notes for the ARPANET research community. The tone mattered: “Request for Comments,” not “edict.” Jon Postel edited the series for nearly three decades, turning informal memos into the durable public record of how the internet actually works. Door out: the RFC Editor history.

    When the research community outgrew hallway meetings, it grew the Internet Engineering Task Force (IETF). The load-bearing phrase is Dave Clark’s: rough consensus and running code — standards earn their keep by shipping, not by voting. The IETF does not own the internet; it owns a process for arguing protocols into something operators will deploy. Door out: ietf.org.

    Numbers: Someone Has to Hand Out the Identifiers#

    Packets carry protocol numbers, port numbers, address blocks, ASN values, media types — identifiers that are worthless if two groups mint the same one for different meanings. For years that ledger lived in one place: the Internet Assigned Numbers Authority (IANA), run as a practical service by Jon Postel (with Joyce Reynolds as a long-time contact point) under research contracts at UCLA and later USC/ISI. IANA is the seed of a kind: a neutral registry for protocol parameters so implementations can interoperate without bilateral negotiation.

    As the network scaled past one person’s notebook, number resources split by kind. Protocol parameters stayed with IANA. IP address space moved toward Regional Internet Registries — ARIN (1997) for much of North America; RIPE NCC, APNIC, LACNIC, and AFRINIC are the same kind for other regions, with national or local Internet registries (NIRs) sitting under some of them where the region chose that model. The pattern is the point: scarce, unique identifiers need a custodian everyone can point at. Door out: iana.org, arin.net.

    Names: Humans Need Words; Machines Need Uniqueness#

    Paul Mockapetris’s DNS specifications (RFC 1034, RFC 1035) turned hierarchical names into a distributed lookup system on top of the address fabric. Names are political in a way raw numbers are not: people argue over words. Jon Postel’s RFC 1591 framed early domain policy as stewardship.

    By the late 1990s the U.S. government wanted those coordination functions out of a single research contract. ICANN (Internet Corporation for Assigned Names and Numbers) formed in 1998 — interim chair Esther Dyson — to hold the institutional seat for names and numbers policy while IANA continued as the operational registries under that umbrella. Read ICANN as another seed of a kind: a multi-stakeholder body whose job is stability and uniqueness of the shared namespace, not picking winners among applications. Door out: icann.org.

    Encodings: The Payload Has to Mean the Same Glyph#

    Interoperation fails quietly when two ends disagree what a byte sequence is. The Unicode Consortium (founded 1991; early work from Joe Becker, Mark Davis, and others across companies that needed a single character set) maintains the shared mapping from abstract characters to code points — the reason Thai, Latin, and emoji can share one wire without each application inventing its own alphabet table. UTF-8 then became the dominant on-the-wire encoding for that repertoire. Door out: unicode.org, Unicode Standard.

    Kin of the Same Kind#

    A few peers earn a sentence — same kind of shared meaning, not a standards-body tour:

    • W3C and WHATWG — Tim Berners-Lee founded the World Wide Web Consortium (1994) to steward web platform standards; WHATWG later carried the living HTML/DOM specs when the browser vendors needed a faster iteration path. Same kind: who may define what a document and an API mean in the browser.
    • IEEE — IEEE 802 is why Ethernet (Post 40) and Wi-Fi are not proprietary island dialects at the link layer.
    • ISO and peers — formal international standards sit beside the RFC world where procurement, safety, or national bodies demand them; the internet mostly shipped on RFCs first and harmonized later.

    Why This Belongs in the Journey#

    Posts 39–41 are the engineering bet: packets, layers, unreliable substrate. This post is the civics bet underneath them: shared meaning is infrastructure. Without someone (and some process) deciding numbers, names, and encodings, every new network is a private language. The doors above are the primary sources — RFCs, registries, consortium pages.

    Optional side paths from here: how people found things and talked on that shared wire (Post 43), or how wartime crypto forced machines that could keep secrets (Post 66). The main spine stays packets → systems → silicon → attention; this is a widen-the-lens beat you can take or skip.
