# From copying a page to pasting it, pigeons to cables to satellites, relays to machines that ask for attention:

**how we got here.**

An interactive journey — not an encyclopedia. You live through systems and touch them; you do not collect articles.

The spine is one continuous engineering argument: how each era's hard constraint forced the next architecture.

**Mechanical → relays → tubes → transistor → CPU → OS & languages → buses → networks → fabric & scale → GPUs → attention.**

---

## Experience first

Three client-side labs sit on the path so reading becomes doing:

- a minimal 8-bit CPU you can program
- a virtual breadboard for digital logic
- a full x86 Linux environment in the browser

Start where the journey starts if you want the full foundation. Jump later if you already know the early history and care most about modern silicon. Use the labs wherever they appear (or open them standalone).

Optional side paths widen the lens without breaking the spine. Thin indexes live under [Optional Learning Paths](/paths/) — thematic routes, not a second book.

---

## The pattern

When you know why relays gave way to tubes, why tubes gave way to transistors, why single-core frequency scaling hit a wall, and why matrix-multiply throughput became the dominant design constraint of the last decade, modern AI hardware stops looking like a black box. You see the same move repeating: name the constraint, change the architecture — do not merely push harder against the same wall.

That pattern is the real subject of this journey.
