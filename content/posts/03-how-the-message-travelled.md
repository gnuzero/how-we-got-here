Print made a page that could be copied. Now the copied page has to travel.

## Two jobs for the same part

On a telegraph line a relay does a different job. A weak signal arriving over a long wire switches a fresh local current, so the message can go farther. Same family of parts, two jobs. In 1835 at the College of New Jersey (later Princeton), Joseph Henry built an early relay in which a small "intensity" magnet controlled a larger "quantity" magnet. ([Smithsonian Institution Archives catalogue](https://siarchives.si.edu/collections/siris_sic_12484)) David Hochfelder's Joseph Henry Papers essay adds that this intensity-to-quantity arrangement became, with some modification, the basis of Morse's repeater and local receiving circuit. We don't call Henry the sole inventor of the relay. ([Hochfelder, "Joseph Henry: Inventor of the Telegraph?"](https://siarchives.si.edu/sites/default/files/pdfs/jhpp/JHP_Inventor_of_the_Telegraph.pdf))

### Before the wire: messages on wings

Moving a message in hops is older than electricity. Frontinus records that during the siege of Mutina, Hirtius kept pigeons in the dark without food, tied letters to their necks and released them near the city walls; the birds, eager for light and food, sought the highest buildings (*altissima aedificiorum*), where Decimus Brutus collected the letters. The Loeb/Thayer edition dates the episode to 43 BC in note 67. ([Frontinus, *Strategemata* 3.13.8](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/thayer/e/roman/texts/frontinus/strategemata/3*.html))

On 28 April 1850, Paul Julius Reuter started a pigeon service between Brussels and Aachen with 45 birds from Heinrich Geller, an Aachen brewer, baker and pigeon breeder. On 14 June 1851 he arrived in London from Aachen, where he had been running a news and stock-price service that combined telegraph cables and carrier pigeons. Reading the pigeons as covering a telegraph gap is ours, not The Baron's wording. ([The Baron, Reuters history](https://www.thebaron.info/archives/history))

### The electric message

Cooke and Wheatstone patented their telegraph on 10 June 1837. Distant Writing records an hour-long trial of the whole Euston Square–Camden Town circuit on the London and Birmingham Railway on 6 September 1837. ([Distant Writing, Cooke & Wheatstone](https://www.distantwriting.co.uk/cookewheatstone.html)) On 24 May 1844 Morse officially opened the Washington–Baltimore line; Annie Ellsworth chose the message "What hath God wrought," and Alfred Vail returned an identical message from Baltimore. ([U.S. Senate history](https://www.senate.gov/artandhistory/senate-stories/morses-telegraph-in-the-capitol.htm))
