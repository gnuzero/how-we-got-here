Printing separates a reproducible mark from the hand that copies it. Carve or set the characters once, and the page can run again without anyone rewriting it. That is why this page sits in Phase 1, before gears and relays.

## The earliest dated printed book

The British Library holds a woodblock-printed *Diamond Sutra* from Dunhuang (Or.8210/P.2). Its colophon names Wang Jie and is dated the 15th day of the 4th month of the 9th year of Xiantong. The Library converts that to 11 May 868 and calls the scroll the earliest dated printed book. ([IDP / British Library, colophon panel](https://idp.bl.uk/blog/the-diamond-sutra-on-display-text-panel-6-with-colophon/))

Later writers dated official printing of the Classics to the minister Feng Dao. Shen Kuo says printed books were not yet common in the Tang and that the Classics were first printed under Feng Dao. Wang Zhen gives the year: 931, when Feng Dao asked for the Nine Classics to be cut on blocks. That is a claim about *state* printing of the canon, not about the start of printing. ([Shen Kuo, *Mengxi bitan* juan 18](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E5%A4%A2%E6%BA%AA%E7%AD%86%E8%AB%87_(%E5%9B%9B%E9%83%A8%E5%8F%A2%E5%88%8A%E6%9C%AC)/%E5%8D%B7%E5%8D%81%E5%85%AB); [Wang Zhen, *Nongshu* juan 22](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E7%8E%8B%E7%A6%8E%E8%BE%B2%E6%9B%B8/%E5%8D%B7%E4%BA%8C%E5%8D%81%E4%BA%8C))

## Bi Sheng

Everything we know about Bi Sheng comes from one text. In *Mengxi bitan* juan 18, Shen Kuo writes that in the Qingli era (1041–48) a commoner named Bi Sheng made movable type. Each character was cut in clay as thin as the rim of a coin and fired hard. The type was set in an iron frame on an iron plate coated with pine resin, wax and paper ash, then warmed and pressed flat. Two plates took turns: one printed while the next was being set. Common characters had twenty or more copies each. For two or three copies the method was not simple; for tens, hundreds or thousands it was "extremely fast." Bi Sheng did not use wood, because its grain is uneven and it swells when wet. After his death the type passed to Shen Kuo's relatives. The text names no book printed with it, and no Bi Sheng type survives. ([Wikisource, *Mengxi bitan* juan 18](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E5%A4%A2%E6%BA%AA%E7%AD%86%E8%AB%87_(%E5%9B%9B%E9%83%A8%E5%8F%A2%E5%88%8A%E6%9C%AC)/%E5%8D%B7%E5%8D%81%E5%85%AB))

## What survives: Tangut wooden type, and a disputed Song fragment

The oldest surviving movable-type print experts have agreed on is not in Chinese. It is a Tangut-language sutra, *吉祥遍至口和本续*, found in Ningxia. In 1996 an expert panel endorsed Niu Dasheng's finding that it is a wooden-movable-type print from the late Western Xia, the earliest wooden-movable-type print yet found. ([Guangming Daily on Niu Dasheng](https://epaper.gmw.cn/gmrb/html/2012-10/14/nw.D110000gmrb_20121014_1-12.htm))

A Northern Song sutra fragment found in Wenzhou's Baixiang Pagoda in 1965 may be older. The Wenzhou Museum says the experts who studied it have not agreed whether it is movable-type printing, stamped type, or a printed sutra picture, although the museum itself leans toward movable type. We report the dispute and don't pick a winner. ([Wenzhou Museum](https://www.wzmuseum.cn/Art/Art_18/Art_18_3774.aspx))

## Wang Zhen: bring the characters to the worker

At the end of his *Nongshu*, Wang Zhen describes a set of wooden type, more than 30,000 characters, sorted by rhyme onto two large revolving wheels about seven *chi* across. One person sits between them and turns either wheel. In his words: for a person to look for characters is hard; for characters to come to the person is easy. As magistrate of Jingde county he used the type to print the county gazetteer, more than 60,000 characters, and had 100 copies in under a month. The text gives no year for that trial, so we don't either. ([Wikisource, *Nongshu* juan 22](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E7%8E%8B%E7%A6%8E%E8%BE%B2%E6%9B%B8/%E5%8D%B7%E4%BA%8C%E5%8D%81%E4%BA%8C))

## Bronze, metal, scale

Taiwan's National Central Library holds a book printed in 1490 with bronze movable type by Hua Sui's Huitongguan in Wuxi. The library describes it as the earliest surviving bronze-type imprint. ([Bureau of Cultural Heritage, Taiwan](https://twh.boch.gov.tw/world-memory/tw_detail.aspx?id=107))

In Korea, the second volume of *Jikji* was printed with movable metal type at Heungdeok temple in Cheongju in July 1377. UNESCO calls it the world's oldest surviving evidence of movable metal type printing. The one surviving volume is in the Bibliothèque nationale de France. ([UNESCO Memory of the World](https://www.unesco.org/en/memory-world/baegun-hwasang-chorok-buljo-jikji-simche-yojeol-volii-second-volume-anthology-great-buddhist-priests); [BnF](https://www.bnf.fr/en/jikji-treasure-world-printing))

Qing scale closes the Chinese arc. From 1773 the Wuying Hall printing office cut more than 250,000 pieces of jujube-wood type for the *聚珍版* books. Jin Jian's manual sets out the process. The Palace Museum calls it the largest wooden-movable-type printing in history. ([Palace Museum](https://www.dpm.org.cn/ancient/hall/148386.html))

The thread to carry forward: a Latin printer needs a few dozen sorts, a Chinese printer tens of thousands. Storing and finding that set comes back when pages become software.

## At the bottom: Gutenberg

Britannica places Johannes Gutenberg's press in Europe in the 1450s and dates his Bible to 1455. It also says he did not invent printing with movable type, which happened in Korea in the 14th century. That sentence skips Bi Sheng; this page has already put him first. ([Britannica, Johannes Gutenberg](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Johannes-Gutenberg))
